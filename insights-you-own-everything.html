Ownership · August 2026

You own everything.

3 min read

All insights

A client told me something last month that stuck with me. His previous agency built his entire automation stack, then left, and he realised he could not actually move any of it without starting over.

He did not own it. He was renting access to something he thought he had bought.

Here is the plain version of how I think about this. You own everything I build for you. Not a licence to use it. Not access as long as you keep paying. The system, the code, the data, all of it, yours from day one.

What I mean is, if I disappeared tomorrow, nothing about your business would stop working.

That sounds like a low bar. It should not be. Migration costs for AI systems can pass two hundred thousand dollars by year two once prompts, integrations, and memory get tightly coupled to one provider, and the real cost of owning an agentic AI platform often runs two to four times the advertised subscription price once you count what it takes to actually get out.

There is a legal detail most people never think to check. In the US, work built by an independent contractor stays theirs by default, unless the contract has explicit present tense language assigning it to the client. I might be wrong about how many agencies get this wrong on purpose versus by accident, but either way, plenty of businesses have signed something without checking which one they got.

But here is something worth saying plainly. Buyers are already noticing. Seventy seven percent of small and midsize businesses in the US now use AI regularly, and nearly a quarter of CMOs say it has already reduced how much they lean on an outside agency. People are getting more comfortable owning things themselves. The agencies still selling dependency as a feature are going to feel that shift.

Own the content. Rent the containers.

That is the whole model. Fixed price, no lock in, and you walk away owning the thing you paid for, whether you keep working with me or not.

If that is not how your current setup works, it might be worth checking what you actually signed.

Next time I will get into what actually happens once ownership changes hands, and what usually goes wrong when it does not.
